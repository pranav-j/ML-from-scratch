The lighthouse stood at the end of the long grey point, and the sea came up to it
every night with the same slow argument. The keeper was an old man named Aren, and
he had kept the light for thirty winters. He knew the sound of every wind that
crossed the point. He knew the small click the lamp made before it caught, and the
smell of the oil, and the way the stairs turned under his hand in the dark.

Every evening he climbed the stairs. There were a hundred and twelve of them, and
he counted them out of habit, not out of doubt. At the top he opened the small
iron door and let the cold in, and he cleaned the glass with a cloth he kept in
his pocket for that purpose and no other. Then he lit the lamp and sat down on the
wooden stool and watched the beam go out across the water, turning and turning,
the way it had turned before he was born and would turn after he was gone.

The village lay two miles inland, behind the low hills, and the people there did
not think about the light very much. It was simply there, the way the hills were
there, the way the sea was there. But the boats thought about it. On bad nights
the boats came in past the rocks with their lamps down low, and they looked for
the beam, and when they found it they turned toward the harbour and lived.

Aren had a dog once, a small brown dog with one bad ear, and the dog had died in
the spring of a year he no longer bothered to name. After that he kept birds
instead, not in cages but simply by feeding them on the step, and they came in the
morning and went away again, and he thought this was a better arrangement for
everyone. He talked to the birds sometimes. He did not expect an answer and he did
not get one, and this suited him.

In the winter the storms came from the north and the sea went white. The wind
found every crack in the tower and made a low sound in them, a sound like a man
humming with his mouth shut. On those nights Aren did not sleep. He sat by the
lamp with his coat on and he watched the glass and he waited. Twice in thirty
years the lamp had gone out. Both times he had it burning again before the beam
had been dark long enough for anyone to notice, and he never told anyone, and he
thought about it often.

The boy came in the autumn of the thirty-first year. He came up the point in the
afternoon with a canvas bag on his shoulder and his boots wrong for the ground,
and he stood at the bottom of the tower and looked up at it for a long time before
he knocked. Aren opened the door and looked at him and said nothing.

I am to learn the light, the boy said.

Who says so.

The harbour office says so. They say you are old.

They are right about that, Aren said, and he stood back from the door and let the
boy in, and that was the whole of it.

The boy's name was Tomas and he was seventeen and he did not know anything. He did
not know how to trim a wick. He did not know that salt gets into brass and eats it
from the inside if you leave it a week. He did not know that you clean the glass
from the top down, always, so that what you wipe away does not fall onto what you
have already cleaned. He learned these things slowly, and badly, and then well.

Count the stairs, Aren told him.

Why.

Because one night the light will go out and you will be at the bottom and there
will be no light to count them by. Then you will want to know where you are.

Tomas counted the stairs. There were a hundred and twelve of them. He counted them
going up and he counted them going down and after a month he did not have to count
them at all, and he understood then that this was the point, and he did not say so,
and Aren did not say so either, and they went on.

The winter came early that year. The first storm arrived on a Tuesday and stayed
for four days, and the sea threw stones the size of a fist against the door of the
tower, and the wind made its low shut-mouth sound in the cracks. Tomas was afraid
and did not say that he was afraid. Aren knew, the way you know weather.

It will not come down, Aren said. It has been here longer than the storm has.

How do you know.

I do not know. But I have said it for thirty winters and it has not come down yet,
so the words are doing some good somewhere.

On the third night the lamp guttered. Tomas saw it first, the small stutter in the
flame, and he was up off the stool before he had decided to be, and his hands
were on the wick and doing the right things before he had time to think about
which things were right. The flame steadied. The beam went out across the water
and found the black shape of a boat coming in past the rocks, low and slow, and
then the beam turned away and came back and the boat was further in, and then it
was past the rocks and safe.

Aren watched all of this from the stool and did not get up.

You did not help, Tomas said afterward.

You did not need help.

I might have needed help.

You might have. Then I would have helped. But you did not, so I sat, and now you
know that you did not need help, which is a thing you could not have learned any
other way.

The storm went out on the fifth day and the sky came back a thin clean blue and
the sea lay down flat as a plate, the way it does after, as though nothing had
happened and nothing ever would. They walked down the point together in the
morning and looked at what the water had left behind. Wood, mostly. Rope. A boot
with no partner. A crate with a name on it in a language neither of them read.

It always gives some of it back, Aren said. Never the parts you want.

They carried the wood up to the tower and stacked it against the south wall to dry,
and the birds came down and walked about on the stones and looked at them, and
Tomas put out bread without being told to, and Aren saw this and said nothing about
it, and something in the arrangement of the house shifted a little and stayed
shifted.

Spring came. The days got long and the light was needed less and tended more, and
they took the lamp apart in the slack afternoons and cleaned every part of it and
put it back together, and Tomas learned the order of the parts by the feel of them.
The brass was pitted where the salt had got in over the years. Aren showed him the
worst of it, a green bloom on the underside of the housing where nobody would look
unless they knew to look.

That is thirty years of not quite being careful enough, he said. Yours will be
somewhere else. Find it before it finds you.

In the summer a man came from the harbour office with papers, and he sat at the
table and drank the tea he was given and explained that there was to be a new
light on the point, an electric one, on a steel frame, and it would not need a
keeper, only a man to come out twice a year and look at it. He said this kindly.
He had rehearsed it on the way up the point and it had gone better in his head.

When, Aren said.

Two years. Perhaps three.

Aren nodded and looked at the window, where the sea was doing its slow bright
nothing in the afternoon.

Then we have two years, he said. Perhaps three.

The man went away down the point and they watched him go, and Tomas waited for
Aren to say something else about it, and Aren did not, that day or any day after.
They cleaned the glass. They counted the stairs. They lit the lamp at dusk and put
it out at dawn and wrote the hour of each in the book, and the book filled up the
way the books before it had filled up, and went on the shelf with the others.

The second winter was worse than the first. In December the sea came over the low
part of the point and cut the tower off from the road for nine days. They had food
for that and more, because Aren had always kept food for that and more, and they
ate badly and did not complain, and on the sixth day Aren could not climb the
stairs.

He tried. He got to the fortieth and stopped and stood with his hand flat on the
cold stone wall, and his breath went in and out too fast and too shallow, and
Tomas came down to him and did not offer his arm because he knew better than that.

Go up, Aren said.

I will come back down.

Go up. It is nearly dark.

Tomas went up. He lit the lamp. He cleaned the glass from the top down, always,
and he sat on the wooden stool and watched the beam turn out across the black
water, and below him the old man climbed the stairs one at a time, resting at
every fourth, and it took him the better part of an hour, and when he came through
the small iron door he was grey in the face and he sat down on the floor with his
back against the wall.

A hundred and twelve, he said.

I know.

Good, Aren said. Then it does not matter that I am slow.

In March he stopped climbing at all. He sat in the room at the bottom with the
window facing the water, and Tomas kept the light, and in the morning Aren asked
him what the night had been like and Tomas told him, and after a while they both
understood that the telling was the work now, and they did it properly.

The birds still came. Aren fed them from the chair with the door propped open, and
they came right up to his feet, and he said that they had got bold since the winter
and that he did not approve, and he went on feeding them.

He died in the autumn of the thirty-third year, in the afternoon, with the door
open and the sea flat and the light not yet needed. Tomas found him in the chair
and sat down on the step beside him for a long time and did not do anything else.
Then, because it was getting dark, he got up and climbed the stairs and lit the
lamp, because that is what the light is, and there is nobody else to do it, and it
does not wait for you.

The men came out from the village the next day and they took the old man down the
point on a cart. Tomas did not go with them. He stood at the bottom of the tower
with the cloth in his pocket and watched until the cart went behind the low hills,
and then he went up and cleaned the glass, top down, always, and found the green
bloom on the underside of the housing and left it exactly where it was.

The electric light came in the spring of the year after, on a steel frame, at the
very end of the point where the ground is hard. It did not need a keeper. A man
came out twice a year and looked at it and went away again, and the beam it threw
was whiter and further and did not turn with the same patience, and the boats did
not care, because a boat does not care what a light is made of, only that it is
there when it is looked for.

Tomas stayed a year in the old tower and then went inland, and later he went to
sea himself, which surprised everyone but him. On bad nights, coming in past the
rocks with the lamps down low, he would find the white beam and turn toward the
harbour and live, and he would think about the hundred and twelve stairs, and
about a man on the fortieth with his hand flat against the cold stone, counting.


The harbour at Ostgard is shaped like a hand held half open, and the town sits in
the palm of it, and the two long stone arms of the breakwater reach out into the
grey water and do not quite meet. The gap between them is forty feet wide. Every
boat that has ever belonged to Ostgard has come home through that gap, and a good
number have not, and the town keeps both lists, and reads the second one aloud
once a year in the autumn while the candles burn down in the window of the hall.

Mira Halden kept the boatyard on the north arm. Her father had kept it before her
and his mother before him, and the name was painted on the long shed in letters
that had been repainted so many times that the newest paint stood a little proud
of the wood, like a scar that had healed thicker than the skin around it. She was
forty-one and she built two boats a year and mended a great many more, and she was
the only person in Ostgard who could look at a hull in the water and say what was
wrong under it without getting wet.

It is in the way she sits, Mira would say. A boat sits the way a person sits. If
her shoulders are wrong you do not need to see her feet.

The men who brought her work did not always believe this and they always paid her
anyway, because she had been right too many times in a row for the argument to be
interesting.

The boatyard smelled of pitch and cut pine and the particular cold iron smell of
the sea in winter. There was a stove at the back that did not draw properly and
had never drawn properly, and every few years somebody offered to fix it, and Mira
said that she had got used to the smoke and did not want to learn a new stove at
her age, and this was a joke she had been making since she was twenty-six.

Her apprentice that year was a girl from up the coast called Ilse, who had come
down with her uncle's fishing boat in the spring and had simply not gone home
again. She was quick with her hands and slow with her mouth, which Mira thought was
the right way round, and she learned the work the way you learn a language when
you are young enough, which is to say all at once and without noticing.

Do not sand it smooth, Mira told her. Sand it fair.

What is the difference.

Smooth is what your hand says. Fair is what the water says. Close your eyes and
run your palm down the whole length of it and do not stop, and if your hand tells
you a story with no surprises in it, that is fair.

Ilse closed her eyes and ran her palm down the length of the hull, and near the
stern her hand went over a small flat place where the curve stopped being a curve
for the width of two fingers and then started again.

There, she said.

Yes, Mira said. There. Now it is your problem.

The winter that year was long and low and nothing much happened in it, which is
the best kind. The boats came in through the gap in the breakwater and went out
again, and the fish were neither many nor few, and the town read its two lists in
the autumn and the second one did not get any longer, and everyone pretended not
to be counting and everyone was counting.

In February the Sella came in with her bow stove in above the waterline, having
met the north arm in the dark at a speed that nobody wanted to discuss in front of
her owner. They brought her up on the slip and Mira walked around her twice and put
her hand flat on the broken planking and stood there a while with her eyes shut.

Bad, said the owner.

Not bad, said Mira. Expensive. Bad is when I take my hand away and tell you to sell
the engine.

She and Ilse worked on the Sella for five weeks. They cut out the broken planks and
steamed new ones and bent them home one at a time, and the shed filled with white
steam that stank of hot sap and made the cold air outside feel like walking into
water. Ilse burned the inside of her wrist on the steam box in the second week and
did not mention it until Mira saw the blister and made her sit down and be
attended to, ungently.

You will do that once, Mira said, tying the cloth. Everyone does it once. The ones
who do it twice do not stay in the trade, so now we will find out about you.

Ilse did not do it twice.

When the Sella went back in the water in March the whole north arm came out to
watch, because there is nothing else to do in Ostgard in March. She sat right. Her
shoulders were square and her bow came up out of the water the way a bow is
supposed to, and the owner stood on the stone with his hands in his pockets and
his face doing something complicated, and Mira watched the hull and not the man,
because the hull was the part she had made.

Ilse stood beside her and looked at the place where the new planks met the old
ones, which you could see, because you could always see it, because wood that has
been in the sea for eleven years does not match wood that came out of a shed in
February no matter what you do to it.

It shows, Ilse said.

It will always show.

That bothers me.

Good, Mira said. Let it bother you for about a year. After that it will stop
bothering you, and then you will be able to work, and you will also be a little bit
worse than you are now, and there is nothing to be done about that either.

In the summer the yard was full. They had four boats on the hard and a fifth
waiting, and the days ran long and yellow and the gulls stood on the shed roof and
screamed about nothing, and Ilse learned to caulk and to scarf a joint and to set
a plank so that it wanted to lie where you put it instead of having to be argued
into place every inch of the way.

A boat that fights you now will fight the sea later, Mira said. Not because the
wood remembers. Because whatever made it fight you is still in there.

They talked very little while they worked, and a great deal in the half hour after
they stopped, sitting on the slip with their backs against the warm stone and the
tide coming up over the weed, and that was when Mira told her the things that were
not about boats. About the winter her father went through the ice on the inner
harbour and came out again and was never quite the same about water afterward,
though he never once said so. About the two years Mira had spent inland, in a town
with no sea in it, which she described as living in a room with the door shut.
About the second list, and the three names on it she had known.

You could go anywhere, Mira said once. You are good enough now that anywhere would
have you.

I know.

You should think about it seriously and not just say that you know.

I have thought about it seriously, Ilse said, and I am going to stay, and I would
rather you did not make me explain it, because the explaining will come out worse
than the reason.

Mira thought about that and decided it was a fair answer and never raised it again,
which was itself a kind of answer.

The gap in the breakwater is forty feet wide. Somebody proposes closing it about
once a generation, and the harbour office draws up the plans and the town argues
about them for a winter, and in the spring the plans go back in the drawer, because
the gap is where the water breathes. Close it and the harbour goes still and foul
and fills with weed, and everybody knows this, and everybody proposes closing it
anyway, because it is also where the trouble comes in.

You cannot have the one without the other, Mira said. Anyone who tells you that
they have found a way to have the one without the other is selling something, or
is young.

Ilse took over the yard eleven years later, and repainted the name on the long
shed, and the new paint stood a little proud of the old, the way it always had.
She kept the stove that did not draw. She said she had got used to the smoke.


There was a road that ran along the top of the cliffs from Ostgard to the point,
four miles of it, and in the middle of the four miles there was a house with no
one in it. It had been a farm once, when there was still soil enough on the head
to be worth turning, and then the soil had gone off the top of the hill in a bad
spring some seventy years back and had not come again, and the family had gone
inland, and the house had stayed where it was and got on with the business of
falling down slowly.

Children from the town walked out to it in the summer and told each other the
usual things. That a man had hanged himself in the byre. That a light showed in
the upper window on the night before a boat was lost. That if you stood in the
kitchen with your eyes shut and counted to a hundred you would hear somebody come
in behind you and set a pot down on the table. None of it was true and all of it
was useful, in the way that stories about empty houses are useful, which is that
they keep children from going inside empty houses, mostly.

Old Eilif, who carried the post out to the point twice a week for thirty years,
stopped at the house every trip in all weathers, and sat on the step and ate his
bread and looked at the sea, and when people asked him whether he had ever heard
the pot he said that he had not, and that he had never counted to a hundred with
his eyes shut in anybody's kitchen, and that a man who does that has more time than
work.

But he did keep the door shut. Every trip, going out, he found the door swung open
on its one good hinge with the wind coming off the water and going through the
front room and out the broken back window, and every trip he pulled it to and set
the stone against it, and every trip on the way home two days later the stone was
moved and the door was open again. The wind did it. He knew the wind did it. He
shut the door anyway, for thirty years, and could not have told you why, and did
not think it needed explaining.

When Eilif got too old for the road they gave the post to a young man with a
bicycle, and the young man rode past the house without stopping, because there was
no reason to stop, and within two winters the door was off its hinge altogether
and lying in the grass, and after that the house came down quite quickly. A roof
goes when the weather gets under it. Once the roof is going, the rest of it is only
a question of how many winters, and the answer on that coast is never very many.

Eilif walked out to look at it once more the spring before he died. It took him
most of the day and he had to stop twice, and when he got there the walls were down
to shoulder height and the grass had come up inside the rooms, and the doorway was
just a gap between two piles of stone with nothing to be shut. He stood in it for a
while. Then he went home, slowly, along the top of the cliffs, with the sea on his
right hand going about its business the way it does, flat and bright and enormous
and not interested, and he thought that this was, on the whole, a fair arrangement,
and that he had no complaint to make about any of it.

The town still reads the second list in the autumn. The candles are the same
candles they have always used, short and thick and yellow, and they are set in the
window of the hall facing the water, and they burn down over the course of the
evening while the names are read out in the order the years happened. It takes a
little over an hour. Nobody has ever suggested shortening it.

The reading is done by whoever is oldest and willing, which is not always the same
person as whoever is oldest. The year Mira Halden did it she got through the whole
list without stopping, including the three names she had known, and afterward she
went out onto the stone and stood looking at the gap in the breakwater with her
coat open in the cold, and Ilse came out and stood beside her and did not say
anything for a long time.

Forty feet, Mira said at last.

Forty feet, Ilse said.

Then they went back inside, because the candles were still burning and it is bad
manners to leave before they are out, and there was soup, and somebody's child was
asleep under a bench with a coat over it, and the whole room smelled of wet wool
and hot bread, and the sea went on outside in the dark doing what it does, which is
nothing at all and everything, patiently, without once being asked.
